# What to post on social media each week

![Phone on a counter suggesting a quick social post, grayscale](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1511707171634-5f897ff02aa9?auto=format&fit=crop&q=80&w=1200)

**Weekly rhythm** here means a fixed loop of post types so you are not inventing a new format every Sunday night. **The smallest loop that still works** has four beats: a behind-the-scenes moment, proof of work you are proud of, one useful tip tied to what you sell, and one clear note about hours, a sale, or how to book. Line them up in one short sitting, publish them through the week, and pick steady over flashy.

**The part that stings** is not the publish button; it is the empty text box before it. You open the app, stare at it, and close it again while you are also running payroll and answering the phone. Posting feels like one more job with no clear finish line, and it is easy to treat it as optional until your feed goes quiet.

When researchers asked small business decision-makers what eats their marketing time, more than half named posting on social as one of the heaviest tasks, and one they often skip (Constant Contact *Small Business Now*, February 2024). The same story shows up in plain conversation: the hard part is not clicking publish once; it is deciding what to say, again and again.

This article explains why a short menu beats a blank page, when three posts a week are enough, when to add an extra slot, and how four roles repeat weekly so you never start from zero.

## The real question you are trying to answer

You are not really asking which hashtag to use or whether Tuesday beats Wednesday. You are asking how to show up without starting from zero every time. That is a **repeatability** problem, not a talent problem.

Survey after survey of small businesses shows the same squeeze: most owners report less than an hour a day for all marketing work, and more than half say they delay marketing because other fires are louder (Constant Contact *Small Business Now*, February 2024). When posting is the task that eats time and still feels optional, the fix is not “be more creative.” It is to shrink the number of decisions. A weekly rhythm does that by giving you the same four jobs, in the same order, every week.

## When fewer, simpler posts are the right move

Choose the lighter version of the rhythm if you are solo, seasonal, or already quiet online for weeks at a time. In that case, aim for **two or three** posts a week instead of four, but keep the **same categories** so your brain still has a menu.

This path fits when you are honest that you will not sustain daily posting, when your customers mostly find you through word of mouth or search, or when you are rebuilding after a long gap. The point is not volume; it is that a stranger scrolling your profile can tell you are still open and still you. Buffer’s large cross-platform frequency study found that weeks with no posts dragged accounts below their own usual growth pattern, while any posting at all beat going dark (Buffer, 2026). For a stretched owner, that reads as permission to post something simple on purpose instead of waiting for a masterpiece.

## When a slightly richer mix is the right move

Add one extra layer when you already post most weeks and want the feed to feel a little fuller without turning into a studio. Keep the same four post types, and add **one** optional slot: a short “did you know” about your trade, a quick staff hello, or a photo of a new shipment on the shelf.

This path fits retail windows, salons with steady appointment flow, cafés with specials, or any shop where people check social before they walk in. You still rotate the same skeleton so you are not inventing a new format every Sunday night. You are just giving yourself permission to use the optional slot when you have a natural moment in front of you.

## The one thing that matters more than clever captions

**Consistency beats polish** for most local businesses. A steady voice and steady proof that you are open do more than a flashy one-off post that does not match anything else you publish.

That is not a lecture about discipline; it is what owners report about their own time. The same Constant Contact survey found that posting on social was the task most often named as both time-heavy and easy to put off (Constant Contact *Small Business Now*, February 2024). If that sounds familiar, your weekly plan should assume you will be tired. Short beats clever. A clear photo with two sentences often outperforms a paragraph you spent an hour polishing.

## A practical weekly rhythm you can copy

Think in four rotating roles. Each week, hit each role once, in whatever order fits what happened at work that week.

1. **Behind the scenes.** Prep, setup, tools, the mess before the floor opens. A bakery might show dough on the bench; a plumber might show the van stock neatly lined; a salon might show a color bowl mid-mix. You are proving you are real and active.
2. **Proof of work.** Before-and-after, a happy customer (with permission), a finished plate, a repaired corner, a styled cut. You are showing the outcome people buy.
3. **A useful tip.** One sentence of advice tied to what you already do. “How to know your bread is done without a thermometer,” “What to check before you reset a breaker,” “How to make a blowout last two more days.” You are helping, not pitching.
4. **A clear invitation.** Hours change, a sale window, booking opens, you are hiring, you are closed for a holiday. You are removing guesswork.

If you use stories or shorter clips, you can pull from the same four roles; you are not inventing a second system. Batch the writing in one twenty- to thirty-minute block when you already have the photos on your phone from the week.

## Here is what we would do in your shoes

Pick the four roles above, set a recurring calendar note for the same day each week, and write captions like you are texting a regular. If a week gets away from you, post the invitation and one proof-of-work photo before you post nothing. Then get back to the full rotation the next week. Keep the photos on your phone as you work so you are not hunting for assets on Sunday night. ## Frequently asked questions

**How many times a week should a small business post on social media?**  
If you are rebuilding a quiet account, three posts a week using the same four-role rotation is enough. If you can sustain four short posts without burning out, that is a solid default for most local shops.

**Do I need professional photos?**  
No. Clear phone photos of real work beat polished stock that does not look like your place. Light and focus matter more than a fancy camera.

**What if I sell something boring to photograph?**  
Show the paperwork you save people, the checklist you follow, the truck you keep stocked, or a quick “here is what this service actually includes.” Boring to you is often clarifying to a new customer.

**Is it okay to repeat the same kind of post every week?**  
Yes. That is the point. Customers are not memorizing your grid; they are checking whether you look active and sound like the same business they walked into last month.

Pick your four roles, protect one small weekly block to line them up, and treat “good enough and posted” as the win. The people who already like you mostly want proof you are still there, still good at the work, and easy to reach.
